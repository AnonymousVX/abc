============================================================ SECTION 1:
AIM AND BASIC CONCEPTS
============================================================

Q1. What is the aim of this practical? Answer: The aim is to train the
same Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) using Sigmoid, Tanh, and ReLU
activation functions, compare their learning behaviour, and study
gradient flow and vanishing or exploding gradient problems.

Q2. What is an activation function? Answer: An activation function is a
mathematical function applied to a neuron’s output. It introduces
non-linearity, allowing a neural network to learn complex patterns.

Q3. Why do we need activation functions? Answer: Without activation
functions, a network made only of linear layers behaves like one linear
transformation, regardless of how many layers it contains. Activation
functions allow the network to learn non-linear relationships.

Q4. What happens if we do not use an activation function? Answer: The
network becomes a composition of linear operations and cannot learn
complex non-linear patterns effectively.

Q5. Which activation functions are compared in this practical? Answer:
Sigmoid, Tanh, and ReLU.

Q6. What is an MLP? Answer: MLP stands for Multi-Layer Perceptron. It is
a feed-forward neural network containing an input layer, one or more
hidden layers, and an output layer.

Q7. What is a feed-forward neural network? Answer: It is a neural
network in which information moves from the input layer through hidden
layers to the output layer, without feedback connections.

Q8. What is the main purpose of comparing activation functions? Answer:
To understand how different activation functions affect training loss,
accuracy, training time, and gradient flow.

Q9. What is non-linearity? Answer: Non-linearity means the relationship
between input and output is not simply a straight-line relationship. It
helps a network learn complex patterns.

Q10. Are activation functions used only in hidden layers? Answer:
No. They can also be used in output layers. The suitable output
activation depends on the task. In this practical, the final layer
returns logits and CrossEntropyLoss handles the classification
objective.

============================================================ SECTION 4:
SIGMOID ACTIVATION
============================================================

Q45. What is the Sigmoid activation function? Answer: Sigmoid is an
S-shaped activation function that maps any real-valued input to a value
between 0 and 1.

Q46. What is the formula for Sigmoid? Answer: sigmoid(x) = 1 / (1 +
e^(-x)).

Q47. What is the output range of Sigmoid? Answer: It is between 0 and 1,
excluding the exact endpoints for finite inputs.

Q48. What is the derivative of Sigmoid? Answer: If y = sigmoid(x), then
its derivative is y(1-y).

Q49. What are the advantages of Sigmoid? Answer: Its output is bounded
between 0 and 1, and it can be useful when a probability-like output is
needed, especially in a binary classification output layer.

Q50. What are the disadvantages of Sigmoid? Answer: It can saturate for
very positive or very negative inputs. In those regions its derivative
becomes very small, which can cause vanishing gradients. Its outputs are
also not zero-centred.

Q51. What does saturation mean in Sigmoid? Answer: Saturation occurs
when the input is very large or very negative, causing the output to
become close to 1 or 0. The derivative then becomes close to zero.

Q52. Is Sigmoid zero-centred? Answer: No. Its outputs are between 0 and
1, so they are not centred around zero.

Q53. Where is Sigmoid commonly used? Answer: It is commonly used in the
output layer of binary classification models to produce a value between
0 and 1.

============================================================ SECTION 5:
TANH ACTIVATION
============================================================

Q54. What is Tanh? Answer: Tanh, or hyperbolic tangent, is an activation
function that maps real-valued inputs to values between -1 and 1.

Q55. What is the output range of Tanh? Answer: Between -1 and 1,
excluding the exact endpoints for finite inputs.

Q56. What is the formula for Tanh? Answer: tanh(x) = (e^x - e^(-x)) /
(e^x + e^(-x)).

Q57. What is the derivative of Tanh? Answer: If y = tanh(x), its
derivative is 1 - y^2.

Q58. Is Tanh zero-centred? Answer: Yes. Its output range is symmetric
around zero.

Q59. What are the advantages of Tanh? Answer: It is zero-centred and can
represent both positive and negative activations.

Q60. What are the disadvantages of Tanh? Answer: For large positive or
negative inputs, it saturates near 1 or -1. Its derivative becomes very
small, so it can suffer from vanishing gradients.

Q61. How is Tanh different from Sigmoid? Answer: Sigmoid outputs values
between 0 and 1, whereas Tanh outputs values between -1 and 1. Tanh is
zero-centred, while Sigmoid is not.

Q62. Why can Tanh sometimes train better than Sigmoid? Answer: Its
zero-centred output can make optimization easier in some networks.
However, Tanh can still suffer from vanishing gradients.

============================================================ SECTION 6:
RELU ACTIVATION
============================================================

Q63. What is ReLU? Answer: ReLU stands for Rectified Linear Unit. It
outputs zero for negative inputs and returns the input itself for
positive inputs.

Q64. What is the formula for ReLU? Answer: ReLU(x) = max(0, x).

Q65. What is the output range of ReLU? Answer: From 0 to positive
infinity.

Q66. What is the derivative of ReLU? Answer: It is 0 for negative inputs
and 1 for positive inputs. At zero, the derivative is conventionally
assigned a subgradient by the implementation.

Q67. What are the advantages of ReLU? Answer: It is simple and
computationally efficient. For positive inputs its derivative is 1,
which helps reduce the vanishing-gradient problem in those active units.

Q68. What is the dying ReLU problem? Answer: A ReLU neuron can become
inactive if it receives negative inputs consistently. Its output and
gradient remain zero, so it may stop learning.

Q69. Does ReLU suffer from vanishing gradients? Answer: For positive
inputs, its derivative is 1, so it avoids the saturation-related
vanishing gradient seen in Sigmoid and Tanh. However, gradients are zero
for negative inputs, and other network factors can still cause small
gradients.

Q70. What are the disadvantages of ReLU? Answer: It can produce inactive
or dead neurons for negative inputs, and its output is not bounded
above.

Q71. Why is ReLU widely used in hidden layers? Answer: It is
computationally simple and often allows deep networks to train
effectively.

Q72. What are alternatives to ReLU? Answer: Leaky ReLU, Parametric ReLU
(PReLU), ELU, and GELU are examples.

COMPARISON OF ACTIVATION FUNCTIONS
============================================================

Q73. Compare Sigmoid, Tanh, and ReLU. Answer: Sigmoid outputs values
between 0 and 1 and can suffer from vanishing gradients. Tanh outputs
values between -1 and 1 and is zero-centred, but can also suffer from
vanishing gradients. ReLU outputs zero for negative inputs and the input
itself for positive inputs; it is efficient but can have dead neurons.

Q74. Which activation function is usually preferred in hidden layers?
Answer: ReLU is a common default choice for hidden layers because it is
simple and often trains efficiently. The best choice still depends on
the task and architecture.

Q75. Which activation function is zero-centred? Answer: Tanh.

Q76. Which activation function has output between 0 and 1? Answer:
Sigmoid.

Q77. Which activation function has output between -1 and 1? Answer:
Tanh.

Q78. Which activation function can produce outputs greater than 1?
Answer: ReLU.

Q79. Which activation functions can suffer from saturation? Answer:
Sigmoid and Tanh.

Q80. Which activation function has a zero output for all negative
inputs? Answer: ReLU.

Q81. Is ReLU always the best activation function? Answer: No. It is
often a strong default for hidden layers, but the best activation
depends on the model, data, and task.

Q108. What is gradient norm? Answer: Gradient norm is a measure of the

Q110. What does param.grad.norm(2) calculate? Answer: It calculates the

Q111. What does a very small gradient norm indicate? Answer: It may
indicate weak gradient updates. If gradients become very small in

Q112. What does a very large gradient norm indicate? Answer: It may

Q113. Does a single average gradient norm prove vanishing gradients?
Answer: No. It is only a summary measure. Layer-wise gradients and their

Q114. What gradient measure is calculated in the original uploaded

fc3, instead of combining all parameter gradients into one average.

Q116. Why is layer-wise gradient measurement useful? Answer: It helps
compare gradient magnitudes at different depths of the network and can

============================================================ SECTION 10:
VANISHING AND EXPLODING GRADIENTS
============================================================

Q121. What is the vanishing gradient problem? Answer: It occurs when
gradients become extremely small as they are propagated backward through
a network. Earlier layers then receive very small updates and learn
slowly.

Q122. Why do vanishing gradients occur? Answer: During backpropagation,
gradients are multiplied by derivatives from multiple layers. If many of
these derivatives are small, the resulting gradient can shrink rapidly.

Q123. Which activations are commonly associated with vanishing
gradients? Answer: Sigmoid and Tanh can cause vanishing gradients when
their inputs are in saturated regions, where their derivatives are close
to zero.

Q124. Why does Sigmoid cause vanishing gradients? Answer: Its derivative
becomes close to zero when its output is near 0 or 1. Multiplying many
small derivatives during backpropagation can make gradients very small.

Q125. Why can Tanh cause vanishing gradients? Answer: Its derivative
becomes close to zero when its output is near -1 or 1. This can shrink
gradients through multiple layers.

Q126. What is the exploding gradient problem? Answer: It occurs when
gradients become excessively large during backpropagation, causing very
large and unstable parameter updates.

Q127. What causes exploding gradients? Answer: Repeated multiplication
by large derivatives or large weights across layers can cause gradient
magnitudes to grow rapidly.

Q128. What are the effects of exploding gradients? Answer: Training can
become unstable, loss may become extremely large or turn into NaN, and
model parameters may change drastically.

Q129. How can vanishing gradients be reduced? Answer: ReLU-like
activations, suitable initialization, normalization methods, residual
connections, and architectures designed for stable gradient flow can
help.

Q130. How can exploding gradients be controlled? Answer: Gradient
clipping, careful weight initialization, suitable learning rates, and
normalization techniques can help control exploding gradients.

Q131. What is gradient clipping? Answer: Gradient clipping limits
gradient magnitude to a chosen threshold before the optimizer updates
the parameters.

Q132. Does ReLU completely solve vanishing gradients? Answer: No. ReLU
has derivative 1 for positive inputs, which helps, but gradients can
still vanish due to inactive units or other network and optimization
factors.

Q133. What is the difference between vanishing and exploding gradients?
Answer: Vanishing gradients become extremely small and cause slow
learning in earlier layers. Exploding gradients become extremely large
and cause unstable updates.

Q134. Are vanishing and exploding gradients the same as overfitting?
Answer: No. They are different problems. Vanishing and exploding
gradients concern gradient magnitudes during training, while overfitting
occurs when a model learns training data too closely and generalizes
poorly.

TRAINING SPEED AND TIMING
============================================================

Q155. What is training speed? Answer: Training speed describes how
quickly a model completes training or processes training data. It can be
measured using elapsed time, time per epoch, or samples processed per
second.

Q156. Does the original uploaded notebook measure actual training time?

batches, and calculate the elapsed time after the training batches
finish.

Q158. Why should validation time be excluded if we want training time?

Q159. Why is torch.cuda.synchronize() used before timing GPU work?

queued GPU operations to finish so that the measured elapsed time is
more accurate.

Q160. What is epoch time? Answer: It is the time taken to complete one
epoch of training.

Q161. What is total training time? Answer: It is the sum of the training
times for all epochs.

Q162. Can we say ReLU is always the fastest? Answer: No. ReLU is
computationally simple, but actual measured time depends on hardware,
software, implementation, and training behaviour. We should report the
measured results.

Q163. How can training speed be compared fairly? Answer: Use the same
architecture, dataset, batch size, number of epochs, optimizer, learning
rate, hardware, and timing method for all activation functions.

Q236. Explain the complete practical in your own words. Answer: In this
practical, I compared three activation functions: Sigmoid, Tanh, and
ReLU. I used the MNIST handwritten digit dataset, which contains
grayscale images of digits from 0 to 9. Each image is 28 by 28 pixels.

average gradient norm. I then plotted these values to compare the three
activation functions. In the improved version, I also measured epoch

Finally, I discussed vanishing gradients, which occur when gradients

become excessively large.

Q237. What conclusion can you give for this practical? Answer:
Activation functions have an important effect on neural-network
training. Sigmoid and Tanh can suffer from vanishing gradients in
saturated regions. ReLU often trains efficiently because its derivative
is 1 for positive inputs, although it can suffer from dead neurons. The
most suitable activation function should be selected by comparing actual
loss, accuracy, gradient behaviour, and training time.
